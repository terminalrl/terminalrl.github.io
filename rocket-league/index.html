Terminal: a self-play Rocket League bot

Sparse-reward reinforcement learning from replay-anchored starts, for 1v1, 2v2 and 3v3

TerminalRL

Terminal is a Rocket League bot trained by self-play reinforcement learning with a sparse reward: +1 for a goal, -1 for conceding. I train an entity-attention policy in RocketSim, starting 70% of episodes from human ranked-replay states and advancing through a rank curriculum. Scaling the batch per update, and kickstarting a larger network from a smaller teacher, were the largest gains. The 1v1 policy scores 27.5 goals and concedes 0.7 per five-minute game against Nexto, a well-known public bot, and 2v2 and 3v3 policies beat Nexto and other public bots as well. The bot was entered in the RLBot Championship 2026.

Method

Environment and observation

I use RocketSim v3 (Rust), wrapped as my own vectorised environment. Physics runs at 120 Hz. The policy acts every 8 ticks (15 Hz) and picks one of 90 discrete actions (the RLGym lookup table). The observation is egocentric (in the car frame, mirrored for orange): the ball, the own car, the other car(s), all 34 boost pads with respawn timers, and the previous controls.

Policy

The actor is an entity-attention network. Tokens are the own car (the query), the ball, each other car (allies and enemies) and the 34 boost pads. Pre-norm attention blocks feed an action head, and a separate attention network is the critic. The deployed 1v1 network has 7.1M parameters (d=256, 4 blocks, 8 heads). Team modes use the same architecture with ally and enemy tokens; absent cars are masked. One policy is used per mode, and it plays greedily (argmax).

Reward

The reward is sparse: +1 for a goal and -1 for conceding, with a discount half-life of 10 s (gamma = 0.51/150 ≈ 0.9954 per step). The final recipe has no shaped or mechanic rewards.

Start states and curriculum

70% of episodes start from human ranked replays: 1v1 duels from ballchasing.com and a public Kaggle set, about 4.3M states, mostly Grand Champion and Supersonic Legend, grouped by time before a goal. 15% are kickoffs and 15% are random. A rank curriculum unlocks higher-rank replay states (gold up to SSL) as the policy passes gates against fixed bots.

Optimisation

PPO in PyTorch, data-parallel across GPUs. The policy plays self-play against a pool of past snapshots with prioritised fictitious self-play (PFSP). Scale was the biggest lever: growing the batch per update from 131k to 524k to 5.2M to 21M agent-steps each gave large gains at equal experience. Team policies train at 168M per update on 4 GPUs (about 1.2M samples/s).

Kickstarting and a human prior

The 7.1M 1v1 network was trained from scratch with a KL term pulling it toward a smaller teacher trained at 21M batch, annealed to zero. It surpassed the teacher (Elo about 1310 versus about 1110). I also tried behaviour cloning on replays, with actions inferred from consecutive states. On its own it plays at random level. As a starting point with a fading KL anchor, it gave the 7.1M network a head start of about 4B steps over training from scratch (Elo 1204 at 10B versus 782).

The deployed 1v1 network has played about 20B agent-steps (about 21 years of game time, about 46 years counting its teacher's lineage) in about 46 h of wall-clock on 2 GPUs.

Nexto (2022, by the RLGym team) is the best-known public Rocket League bot: trained with reinforcement learning on roughly 250,000 hours of gameplay, it was estimated to play at about the level of the top 1% of human players. Limitations

  • Trained entirely in RocketSim; the policies transfer to the real game without changes.
  • Play is deterministic (argmax), so it can be predictable.
  • The policies rarely do advanced aerial mechanics: air dribbles and flip resets almost never happen (well under once per 100 minutes).
  • They run low on boost much more than strong humans do.
  • Evaluation opponents are public bots and my own checkpoints, not human players.
